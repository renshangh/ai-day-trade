The Indium Phosphide Floor

Every optical link in an AI data center begins with a photon that silicon cannot emit. That photon is born in a crystal grown from indium and phosphorus — and AXT is one of three companies on earth that grows it at scale. What that does and does not make the company worth.

The short answerWhere the premise holds and where it breaks

AXT is not the foundation of photonics. It is one of three floors under it — and the floor is currently the constraint.

The physics half of your premise is solid and under-appreciated. Silicon has an indirect bandgap: it computes beautifully and emits light terribly. Germanium is the same. Gallium arsenide emits, but only in the near infrared, where glass fiber is roughly ten times lossier than at the wavelengths carriers actually use. The only commercially proven material system that emits coherent light at 1310 nm and 1550 nm — the two low-loss windows of silica fiber — is the family of alloys lattice-matched to indium phosphide. Every 800G and 1.6T transceiver, every co-packaged optics engine, every coherent long-haul line card and every erbium-amplifier pump laser in the world starts as an epitaxial stack grown on an InP wafer. Silicon photonics does not remove that dependency; it relocates it, because a silicon photonic die still needs an InP laser bonded or coupled to it.

The company half of your premise needs three corrections. First, AXT is number two, not number one: Sumitomo Electric is generally credited with the largest share, and AXT plus Sumitomo together are put at roughly 80% of global InP substrate output, with JX Advanced Metals around 10%. Second, AXT sells the wafer, not the chip — it sits two layers below the transceiver and captures a small slice of the value of the systems it enables. Third, and most importantly, AXT's substrates are all manufactured in China, and China put InP substrates on its export control list on 4 February 2025. Every overseas shipment now requires a permit from Beijing's Ministry of Commerce. Management has called those permits "the most significant challenge we currently face."

The thesis in one line

The scarcity is real, physical, and durable through at least 2027. AXT's leverage to it is real and now visible in the numbers. What is not settled is whether a company whose entire production base sits inside an export-control regime it does not control can convert a genuine bottleneck into durable per-share value before Sumitomo, JX and a state-backed Chinese cohort widen the bottleneck.

Part I — The physicsWhy the photon must be born on InP

Three material constraints stack up, and only one crystal satisfies all three.

Constraint 1 — The emitter must have a direct bandgap

In an indirect-bandgap semiconductor, an electron and hole cannot recombine into a photon without also involving a lattice vibration. The process is orders of magnitude less probable than the competing non-radiative paths, which is why nobody has ever shipped a practical silicon laser. Direct-bandgap III–V compounds recombine radiatively as a matter of course. This single fact is the reason a $50 billion optics industry depends on a handful of crystal growers.

Constraint 2 — The emission wavelength must land in the fiber's low-loss windows

Silica fiber has two useful transmission windows: the O-band around 1310 nm, where chromatic dispersion is near zero, and the C/L-bands around 1550 nm, where attenuation bottoms out near 0.19 dB/km. At 850 nm — where GaAs-based VCSELs emit — loss is over ten times higher, which is why 850 nm links are confined to a few hundred meters of multimode fiber inside a rack row. Scale-out links between racks and across a data center hall need the 1310/1550 windows.

Constraint 3 — The emitting alloy must be lattice-matched to an available substrate

You cannot simply grow the alloy you want on whatever wafer is cheap. A mismatch of even a fraction of a percent between the crystal lattice of the epitaxial film and the substrate beneath it generates threading dislocations, and dislocations are exactly what kills laser lifetime. So the substrate choice determines the alloy palette, and the alloy palette determines the wavelength. This is the crux of the whole argument:

Table 1 — Why the substrate choice fixes the wavelength
Substrate Bandgap Transition Lattice-matched emitter alloys Accessible wavelengths
SiliconSi 1.12 eV Indirect — none that emit efficiently No practical laser
GermaniumGe 0.66 eV Indirect Used as a detector and as a solar-cell base No practical laser
Gallium arsenideGaAs 1.42 eV Direct AlGaAs, InGaAs (strained) 780 – 1100 nmshort-reach VCSEL, sensing, pumps
Indium phosphideInP 1.34 eV Direct InGaAsP, InGaAlAs, InGaAs 1200 – 1650 nmO, E, S, C and L telecom bands

The bottom row is the whole story. Because In0.53Ga0.47As is lattice-matched to InP at a bandgap of about 0.75 eV, and quaternary InGaAsP/InGaAlAs can be tuned continuously between that and InP's own 1.34 eV, a single substrate platform covers the entire telecom spectrum with defect-free epitaxy. No other commercially available substrate does. Attempts to reach 1310 nm from GaAs (InGaAsN, GaAsSb) have been researched for twenty-five years and have never displaced InP in volume.

The fourth, quieter constraint: electron velocity

InP's electron mobility is roughly an order of magnitude higher than silicon's, and its peak electron velocity is higher than GaAs's. That is why InP is not only the light source but also the platform for the electro-absorption modulator integrated alongside the laser in an EML, for InP HBT and HEMT circuits running above 100 GHz, and for the high-speed PIN and avalanche photodiodes at the receive end. As per-lane rates move from 100G to 200G PAM4 and beyond, the modulator bandwidth requirement pushes further into territory where InP is the only proven answer for the highest-performance parts.

Silicon photonics and CPO make the dependency worse, not better

The common misreading is that silicon photonics displaces III–V. It does not. A silicon photonic die can guide, split, modulate and detect light; it cannot generate it. So every silicon photonics platform pairs the silicon die with an InP gain chip — flip-chip attached, micro-transfer-printed, or coupled from a separately packaged external laser source. Co-packaged optics goes further: it moves the optical engine onto the switch package, multiplies the number of independent laser sources per package, and tightens the reliability and low-defect requirements on the substrate because a failed laser inside a switch package is far more expensive than a failed pluggable. TSMC's COUPE platform, Broadcom's and NVIDIA's CPO products all still need an InP light source per package. More integration, more lasers, more wafers.

Why the substrate itself is not a commodity

A wafer is not just a disc of the right compound. Laser-grade InP has to hit dislocation etch-pit densities in the hundreds to low thousands per cm², surface roughness below about 0.3 nm Ra, and crystal-plane orientation tolerances around ±0.01°. It comes in two electrically distinct flavours that are not interchangeable: sulphur-doped n-type for laser and detector epitaxy, and iron-doped semi-insulating (resistivity of order 10⁷ Ω·cm) for high-frequency devices where substrate conductivity would destroy the circuit. AXT grows its crystals by vertical gradient freeze — a slow, low-thermal-gradient technique that yields lower dislocation density than pulling from a melt, at the cost of long growth cycles. Reported single-crystal yields across the industry sit at roughly 15–25%; from 100 kg of high-purity indium feedstock, on the order of 10 kg emerges as saleable wafer. That yield curve, plus the multi-quarter customer qualification cycle for a new substrate source, is the moat. It is also why capacity cannot be conjured in two quarters no matter how much capital arrives.

Part II — PositionSix layers from crystal to cluster

AXT owns the bottom two layers, and only the bottom two.

This is the discipline the premise most needs. "Foundational" and "valuable" are different claims, and the distance between them is measured in layers of the stack. Reading bottom-up, in the order material actually moves:

01 Raw materials AXT is here. High-purity gallium and germanium, InP polycrystalline base material, and pyrolytic boron nitride crucibles, via the JinMei and BoYu subsidiaries. Indium itself is bought in — it is a zinc-refining by-product, and China produced ~69–70% of the world's refined indium.
02 Substrate AXT is here. VGF crystal growth, slicing, lapping, polishing into 2″/3″/4″ and now 6″ InP wafers, plus GaAs and Ge. Three suppliers worldwide at scale. Q2/26 InP revenue: $30.7M.
03 Epitaxy MOCVD growth of the quantum-well laser structure on the substrate. Merchant epi houses (LandMark, VPEC) plus in-house lines at the integrated laser makers.
04 Laser and detector die Fabrication, facet coating, burn-in and screening. Coherent, Lumentum, Sumitomo, Mitsubishi, Broadcom, Source Photonics, Accelink, and the Chinese laser cohort.
05 Optical module / engine 800G and 1.6T pluggables, and co-packaged optical engines. Innolight, Eoptolink, Coherent, Marvell DSP-paired designs, TSMC COUPE.
06 Switch and cluster NVIDIA, Broadcom, Arista, and the hyperscalers who ultimately create the demand signal and who have begun reaching down five layers with prepayments.

The value-capture asymmetry. A 1.6T module sells for four figures. The InP substrate content underneath it is a small fraction of that. AXT's entire trailing-twelve-month revenue is ~$126M against an optical module market measured in tens of billions. The company's leverage is not share of wallet; it is pricing power in a shortage. That is a real but conditional source of value: it works while the bottleneck holds, and it is exactly what everyone above layer 02 is now spending capital to eliminate.

Part III — The companyWhat the numbers now show

Five quarters ago InP was 20% of revenue. Last quarter it was 65%, and the gross margin nearly tripled.

AXT is not a pure InP company on paper — it runs three substrate lines plus a raw-materials arm — but it has become one in practice. The mix shift is the single clearest fact in the financials.

Two things in that table matter more than the headline growth. The operating leverage is severe in both directions: gross margin ran from 8.2% to 45.0% in four quarters on a revenue base that merely went from $18M to $48M, because crystal growth is a high-fixed-cost process where utilisation is everything. And the non-InP business is flat to shrinking. GaAs, which serves 850 nm VCSELs, micro-LED, high-brightness LED and RF power amplifiers, has hovered around $5–7.5M for years. Germanium — substrates for multi-junction space solar cells — has effectively rolled off, from $1.5M to $0.3M a quarter. There is no second engine here. The thesis is InP or nothing.

Vertical integration is a genuine, underrated edge. Through JinMei, AXT purifies its own 6N/7N gallium and produces InP polycrystalline base material; through BoYu it makes the pyrolytic boron nitride crucibles that the crystal growth itself happens inside — a consumable that constrains competitors and that AXT also sells to them. In a market where the raw-material chain (indium, gallium, germanium, pBN) is itself concentrated in China and subject to export controls, owning those steps is a cost and a continuity advantage. It is also why the raw-materials segment grew to $10M last quarter: the whole compound-semiconductor industry is bidding for the same inputs.

Part IV — The marketA 70% shortfall and a 250% price move

Lumentum's CEO said the InP shortage is worse than DRAM or NAND. The capacity numbers support him.

At the RAISE Summit in Paris on 8 July 2026, Lumentum CEO Michael Hurlston said the InP supply–demand imbalance had surpassed that of DRAM and NAND, that his shipments were running more than 30% short of customer demand for EMLs and pump lasers, and that customer order sizes had moved from "hundreds of units typical of traditional telecom customers to hundreds of millions." Lumentum is reported sold out through 2028 despite quadrupling output. Third-party industry estimates put 2025 global InP demand near 1.5–2.0 million wafer-equivalents against 600,000–700,000 of capacity — a gap of roughly 70%. Following China's February 2025 export controls, the average price of a 6-inch InP wafer is reported to have risen about 250%, to roughly $5,000.

Treat the volume figures with care. The 2M-versus-600k comparison circulates widely but the underlying unit ("pieces", "devices", "wafer-equivalents") is inconsistent across sources, and the diameter mix is unstated — a 6-inch wafer yields roughly 400 laser die against about 250 for a 3-inch, so wafer counts and die counts tell different stories. The direction is corroborated by independent evidence: prepayments, sold-out positions, permit-gated backlog, and simultaneous multi-year expansions by every incumbent. The precise multiple is not reliable.

Coherent is a customer, not a substrate rival — a distinction the trade press routinely blurs. Coherent grows its own SiC and GaAs crystals, but it does not grow InP: it buys InP substrates externally under three-to-five-year supply agreements — the AXT agreement in Table 4 is one of them — and performs epitaxy and device fabrication in-house. Its widely reported "6-inch InP" milestones and the $650M Sherman, Texas expansion with NVIDIA are device wafer capacity, quadrupling wafer output at the layer above. That expansion therefore increases substrate demand rather than adding competing supply. The same applies to IQE, which appears on many InP vendor lists but is an epitaxy house.

Market-share figures disagree across sources and should be held loosely. Reuters-sourced reporting puts AXT and Sumitomo together near 80% with JX around 10%, and separately credits Sumitomo with roughly 60% — implying AXT near 20%. Chinese industry sources put AXT as high as 35%. The consistent, decision-relevant fact is the structure, not the split: three incumbents hold 80–90%, and the barrier is yield and qualification rather than capital, since roughly $60M reportedly buys 400,000 wafer-equivalents of nameplate capacity but not the yield to run it.

The listed universe is almost empty

An unusual consequence of this market's structure: the bottleneck is one of the most discussed in AI infrastructure, and there is essentially one liquid listed way to own it directly. Of the companies that actually grow InP crystals, one is a US small-cap, two are Japanese conglomerates where InP is immaterial to the P&L, and the rest are Chinese A-shares or private.

Two names commonly mistaken for members of this list are not: Coherent (COHR) buys its InP substrates and fabricates devices, and IQE (LSE: IQE) is an epitaxy house that also buys substrates. Both are exposure to the layer above. The genuine adjacent vehicles — Lumentum, Coherent, Fabrinet, and the MOCVD toolmakers Veeco and Aixtron — express the same optical-bottleneck thesis, but they are on the demand side of the substrate, not the supply side.

The 4-inch to 6-inch transition is the real competitive battleground

A 6-inch wafer yields on the order of 400 usable laser die against roughly 250 for a 3-inch, and reported cost per die falls to about 60–70% of the 3-inch level. Whoever qualifies 6-inch at production yield first gets a structural cost advantage and relieves the shortage fastest. AXT's 6-inch program is explicitly in "development / pilot production" — that is the company's own characterisation in the Coherent agreement filing, alongside named risks of lower-than-expected yields and difficulty scaling crystal growth. Coherent already runs a 6-inch device line in Texas and will consume 6-inch substrates from whoever can qualify them; on the substrate side, Sumitomo's expansion is a 6-inch line upgrade with mass production reported for 2028. This is the single most important execution variable in the story.

Part V — Risk registerRanked by how much of the thesis each one breaks

The largest risk is not competition. It is that a US-listed company's entire output is gated by permits from Beijing.

China export permitsStructuralHighest

China added InP substrates to its export control list on 4 February 2025. GaAs and germanium went on in August 2023. Every overseas customer order requires a separate MOFCOM permit; processing has run around 60 business days, and there were effectively no InP permits for months before the first was granted in late June 2025. This is the mechanism behind AXT's own revenue volatility — Q3/25 InP hit $13.1M, then Q4/25 fell to $8.0M purely on permit timing, and Q4/25 guidance was missed for the same reason. Management calls permits "the most significant challenge we currently face." It has also hit customers directly: Taiwanese epi houses VPEC and LandMark reported disruption from AXT permit delays. North American revenue fell from 8% to 2% of sales during the worst of it. AXT does not control this variable and cannot forecast it.

Geographic and listing structureStructural

All substrate and raw-material production is in the PRC. Tongmei withdrew its Shanghai STAR Market IPO application on 26 June 2026 and pivoted to a Hong Kong listing, which triggered redemption rights on roughly RMB 324.4M raised from eleven Chinese private equity funds; AXT says it can repay while negotiating their participation in the new listing. Noncontrolling and redeemable noncontrolling interests in Tongmei stood at about 14.5%. A US-listed holding company whose value is a Chinese operating subsidiary being separately listed in Hong Kong is a structure that can compress the parent's multiple regardless of operating performance — and it sits on the wrong side of both governments' escalation options.

The bottleneck attracts the capital that ends itCyclical

Every incumbent is expanding simultaneously — Sumitomo to 3.1× FY2024 capacity, JX on a multi-year program — while $5,000 wafers and Beijing's permit regime give hyperscalers, governments and customers overwhelming incentive to finance non-China supply. Chinese domestic producers, meanwhile, are being pulled forward by the same export controls that constrain AXT's exports: Yunnan Germanium's shipments rose 74% in 2025 and China is targeting 30% global share by 2030. Shortage economics are not a permanent state; the question is whether 2028 capacity overshoots 2028 demand.

Customer concentration, now contractualElevated

The prepayment agreements that de-risk capacity also concentrate the customer base. Lumentum, Coherent and Casela now have contractual claims on reserved capacity, and all three are laser makers exposed to the same end demand — so their orders correlate rather than diversify. If any one of them qualifies an alternative source or slows, the reserved capacity does not simply resell at the same price.

Execution: 6-inch yield and the rampElevated

AXT is attempting to triple InP output in 2026 and roughly double again in 2027, in a process where industry single-crystal yields are 15–25% and where new capacity requires customer requalification. The 6-inch program is pilot-stage by the company's own filing. Missing the ramp does not just cost revenue — at 45% gross margin driven by utilisation, it costs margin non-linearly, in the same direction that produced the 8.2% trough in Q2/25.

Technology substitutionLow near-term

Nothing on the horizon removes the need for a III–V gain material at 1310/1550 nm. The credible long-run threats are cost-structure attacks rather than replacements: InP-on-GaAs engineered substrates (claimed large cost savings and easier 6-inch scaling), quantum-dot lasers grown directly on silicon, and thin-film lithium niobate modulators — the last of which still needs an InP laser in front of it. Treat this as a 2030s risk to substrate volume per link, not a near-term threat to the platform.

Valuation and dilutionElevated

The stock went from under $3 to $143.16 in May 2026 and trades near $60.86 — roughly 58% below the high, still up many multiples year on year. Trailing revenue is $126M against a $3.91B market capitalisation; the forward P/E of ~38 depends entirely on the ramp landing. The April equity raise added shares at the top of the move, insiders including the CEO sold into strength in early June, and the stock has repeatedly fallen on good news, which is what a crowded momentum position looks like. Price discovery here has run several quarters ahead of the financial statements.

Part VI — FalsifiersWhat would settle the open questions

Four checkpoints, each of which moves the thesis rather than decorating it.

Q3 2026 results
~late Oct 2026 Did the ramp land, and did permits allow it? Guidance is ≥$66M with EPS $0.30–0.32 and an InP capacity target of $35–40M. The tell is not the revenue number but the gap between capacity and shipments: if InP capacity hits $40M and InP revenue lands well short, the constraint is permits, not demand — and that gap is the thesis's core vulnerability quantified.
Ongoing Non-China revenue mix. Management guided toward a 40/60 non-China/China split, with China above 50% in Q2/26 on laser-sector demand. Rising non-China share is direct evidence permits are flowing; falling share means the export regime is quietly re-routing AXT into the domestic Chinese market, where prices and competition are worse.
2026 – 2027 6-inch qualification at a named customer. The Coherent agreement runs to June 2029 and is explicitly a development program. Confirmation of production-yield 6-inch shipments would validate both the cost structure and the exit-2027 $130M/quarter target. Continued silence, or a yield disclosure, is the bear signal.
2027 – 2028 Whether the shortage survives the expansions. Sumitomo at 3.1× FY2024, JX's program, China's domestic cohort and AXT's own tripling all arrive in roughly the same window. Watch the 6-inch spot price: if $5,000 holds through 2028 the bottleneck is structural; if it reverts toward pre-control levels, 45% gross margins were a cycle, not a moat.
